Start here — a 5-minute primer

three short tracks · pick the one that matches where you are
TRACK 1

I'm completely new to investing

▶

A share is a small ownership slice of a company that trades on a stock exchange. The price moves with what other people are willing to pay. The Australian Securities Exchange (ASX) is where Australian companies are listed.

A share bought at A$1.00 and sold later at A$1.10 is a 10% profit (before fees and tax); one bought at A$1.00 and sold at A$0.90 is a 10% loss. The dashboard doesn't place trades — it shows statistics about price behaviour.

Most of the dashboard is built around a simple idea: when a share has fallen a long way recently and the data says the fall was driven by sentiment rather than fundamentals, the mean-reversion hypothesis is that prices may partly recover. This is called mean reversion. Measuring which names are most stretched-down is one of the dashboard's main jobs.

Caveat — none of this is financial advice. Share prices can fall as well as rise.

TRACK 2

What this dashboard does

▶

Canetoad watches around 270 liquid ASX-listed materials companies (gold, copper, lithium, uranium, etc.) and computes daily statistics that summarise three things:

  1. What moved today. Sector heat shows the average return of every commodity basket today.
  2. What's been accumulating. Cash flow tracks whether capital is entering or leaving each basket over the past 14 trading days.
  3. What's getting attention. Announcement counts measure unusual news flow per commodity.

The dashboard combines these into mean reversion scores. The pages let you drill from market-wide views down to single tickers, with RSI, drawdown, and correlation metrics on every name.

TRACK 3

How to actually use it day-to-day

▶

A typical morning workflow:

  1. Open /commodities. Look at sector heat — anything bright red or green today?
  2. Cash flow panel — anything changed in the inflow/outflow rankings vs yesterday?
  3. Announcement panel — any commodity with ratio > 2? That's a story breaking.
  4. Mean reversion leaderboard — the top 5 tickers by MR score with RSI ≤ 25 (the most stretched-down names).
  5. Click into any commodity name to drill into per-ticker stats, lead-lag correlations, and the AUDUSD-translated relationships.

The nine worked examples below show this workflow applied to specific historical events. Read them after this primer — that's the bridge from concepts to reading the panels.

Panel reference

every panel on the dashboard, with what it shows, why it matters, and how to read it · clickable from any panel's `?` button

Every panel on the dashboard has a small "?" button next to its title. Click it to see what that panel is for, why it matters, and how to read it. The deep-dives are also collected here for browsing without leaving the Learn page.

Squeeze metrics▾

Detects asymmetric order-book pressure — situations where buyer stacks are heavily outweighing seller depth near the market.

Why it matters

  • Order-book imbalance is different information from recent price return.
  • Measures something price-only indicators like RSI or moving averages don't see.
  • Often read alongside mean-reversion candidates (oversold + heavy bid stack).

How to read it

  • Green ✓ pill = the ticker clears all three thresholds at top-5 levels (imbalance ≥ 3×, ask vacuum ≤ 0.7, order ratio ≥ 2.5×).
  • Green ✓ STRICT pill = clears thresholds at both top-5 AND top-12 — the strictest reading.
  • Amber 'thin' = fewer than 5 levels populated; signal not reliable yet.
  • Score column (0-99): how far past the thresholds the ticker sits, multiplicatively.
  • The ⚙ button sets stricter or looser thresholds.

Caveats

  • Noisy on small-caps (<A$200k/day ADV) — depth distribution can be artefactual.
  • Heavy bid + thin ask can also mean a single buyer defending a support level (not pushing). Best read alongside announcement flow + sector heat.
Live rankings▾

The ML model's daily score for each ASX-materials ticker, sorted by predicted 5-day forward excess return.

Why it matters

  • The top-50 list shows the names the model scores highest for 5-day excess return versus the index.
  • Rankings are recomputed daily — rank #1 today is not necessarily #1 tomorrow.
  • Score is calibrated daily — same score over time has same meaning.

How to read it

  • Rank column: 1 = highest model score.
  • Score: dimensionless model output; higher score = ranked higher by the model.
  • Family: commodity tags this ticker belongs to.
  • 5d / 20d: realised return windows.
  • Vol: 20-day annualised volatility — a high score on a low-vol name carries a different risk profile from a high score on a high-vol one.

Caveats

  • Universe is ASX-materials only (~270 tickers). Doesn't cover financials, REITs, or tech.
Sector heat▾

Today's average percentage move across all tickers tagged with each commodity. The fastest sector read on the page.

Why it matters

  • Names within a sector tend to move together — a strong gold day usually shows up across most gold names.
  • Shows rotations — uranium rising while iron ore falls is meaningful context.

How to read it

  • Tiles are sorted by today's average move, biggest gainers first.
  • Colour ramp: green (positive) → amber (flat) → red (negative).
  • Number under the % shows how many tickers carry that commodity tag.
  • Click a tile to drill into the per-commodity detail page.
Mean-reversion candidates▾

Top stretched-down ASX-materials tickers ranked by composite mean-reversion score (RSI + 30d drawdown + 52w trend filter).

Why it matters

  • Stretched-down (oversold) names are the subject of the mean-reversion hypothesis this panel measures.
  • The 52w trend filter keeps names still in a steep long-term decline (falling knives) off the list.

How to read it

  • Score column (0-100): 50pt RSI(14) component (peaks at RSI≤20) + 25pt 30d drawdown (peaks at -10%, decays past -25%) + 25pt 52w trend filter (zero past -40%).
  • RSI badge — colour-coded oversold (red) → neutral → overbought (green).
  • 60d sparkline gives instant visual confirmation of the recent crash.
  • Δ 52w column highlighted red when stretch is severe (>40% below 52w high) — caution flag.
  • Squeeze column shows the order-book squeeze flag for the same ticker.
Cash flow vs 14d mean▾

Net signed dollar volume per commodity over the last 14 trading days vs. its 60-day baseline.

Why it matters

  • Identifies which sectors are seeing real money inflow vs which are quietly bleeding.
  • 14×60d ratio tells you whether recent flow is above or below typical — a sector ranked +A$2B/14d is more meaningful if 60d-typical is +A$0.5B.
  • Inflows column = sectors where buying is dominating; outflows = where sellers are.

How to read it

  • Bars are sorted by absolute flow descending in each panel.
  • Ratio pill (e.g. 1.5×): recent / 60d-typical. ≥1× = above usual, <1× = below.
  • Daily breakdown sparkline shows whether the flow was steady or one big day.
Announcement activity by commodity▾

Distinct PDF announcements per commodity family over the last 14 days, compared to a 60-day prior baseline. Detects sectors with unusual news flow.

Why it matters

  • Heavy announcement flow often coincides with sector moves — exploration results, M&A, regulatory.
  • Title hits (commodity name in document title) are treated as a stronger indication than a mention in the body.
  • Surfaces sector-specific news cycles.

How to read it

  • Sorted by recent docs descending.
  • Ratio = recent docs ÷ length-normalised prior 60d baseline. >1.5× is a noticeable spike.
  • Title hits column = commodity name appears in document title (stronger signal).
Sector correlations▾

Cross-commodity correlation of market-adjusted daily log-returns (raw return minus ASX200 beta=1). 30d vs 90d windows. Δ surfaces regime shifts.

Why it matters

  • Concurrent correlations show which sectors are moving together right now — useful for understanding diversification.
  • Δ (30d − 90d) catches regime shifts: gold and copper decorrelating, or antimony and tin tightening up.
  • Within-sector R, not broad-market beta — so 'Gold↔Copper +0.85' really means 'gold-specific and copper-specific moves correlate', not 'they both follow the market'.

How to read it

  • Top 10 pairs panel: longest green bars = highest concurrent correlation.
  • 30D R vs 90D R: how the relationship has tightened or loosened recently.
  • Δ (30D − 90D) column with red ↓ / green ↑ icons surfaces the biggest shifts.
  • Persistent / Converging / Decoupling view filters the top changes by direction.
ETF cross-sector correlations▾

How each commodity family correlates with a basket of ASX sector ETFs. Reveals which macro themes are driving individual commodity moves.

Why it matters

  • A high correlation between a commodity basket and a sector ETF means the basket is moving with that broader theme.
  • A low correlation means commodity-specific factors (exploration news, single-name catalysts) are dominating.
  • Shows whether sector beta or stock-specific news is driving moves today.

How to read it

  • Heatmap-style table: rows = commodity, columns = ETFs.
  • Cell colour intensity = magnitude of correlation.
  • Sort by ETF column to find commodities most/least tied to a specific theme.
Top tickers per commodity▾

Per-commodity preview showing the highest-ranked tickers in each commodity tag, with sparklines, RSI badges, 5d return, and squeeze flag.

Why it matters

  • Drill-down view from the heatmap — see exactly which names are driving a sector's move.
  • RSI + sparkline gives instant 'is this name oversold or overbought' read.
  • Squeeze badge shows the order-book squeeze flag inline.

How to read it

  • Tickers sorted by model rank within each commodity.
  • 60d sparkline — quick visual of recent trajectory.
  • RSI badge — colour-coded oversold/neutral/overbought.
  • 5d ret — recent realised return.

Worked examples — 9 real questions, walked through

which panels to open and what to look for in each · this demo includes only the gold commodity page; the other per-commodity pages named below are in the full dashboard
1

Did the dashboard see the uranium run-up coming?

If you'd looked at the dashboard 2 weeks before the price move, would you have seen anything that flagged uranium?

▶
Catalyst

Cameco cut its 2023 production forecasts for Cigar Lake and McArthur River in September 2023, and the uranium spot price rose through late 2023. Large ASX uranium names (PDN, BOE, DYL, NXG) rose about 30–45% between 30 June and 29 December 2023 in the dashboard's price data.

Walk-through — which panels to open

  1. 1

    /commodities → Cash flow

    Uranium in the inflows list. A sparkline trending up from a flat baseline before the headlines would suggest capital was accumulating early.

  2. 2

    /commodities → Announcements

    Uranium ratio (recent docs / 60d baseline) crossing > 1.5 — attention was building. Title-hit count tells you whether uranium was the headline or buried.

  3. 3

    /commodities → Mean reversion leaderboard

    Were uranium tickers (PDN, BOE, DYL, NXG) clustered with high MR + low RSI before the move?

What the chart looks like

What this pattern means
Inference

If flows, attention and MR had all been elevated together before the move, that would describe a broader pattern than any one measure on its own; a single elevated measure on its own is weaker evidence.

Further context
Background

In attention-thin baskets (e.g. tin, antimony) a single announcement can move the whole basket, so the same measures change more abruptly.

2

Why do ASX gold stocks not always track gold?

Are ASX gold stocks really gold exposure, or are they FX-translated USD-gold exposure?

▶
Catalyst

USD gold rose about 18–19% in Q1 2025, its strongest quarter since 1986, while AUDUSD was roughly flat (about 0.62 at both ends of the quarter). Over the same quarter, large ASX gold miners rose anywhere from about 14% (RMS) to 54% (RRL) in the dashboard's price data, so the same gold move showed up very differently from name to name.

Walk-through — which panels to open

  1. 1

    /commodities/gold → Vs commodity spot

    Compare the raw r and translated r columns for NST, EVN, RMS and RRL. The gap between the two shows how much of the measured link to USD gold is currency movement.

  2. 2

    /commodities/gold → Strongest correlations

    AUDUSD bar at lag 0. A negative correlation means a stronger AUD has coincided with weaker ASX gold names: their revenue is set in USD while most of their costs are in AUD. With AUDUSD flat in Q1 2025, this channel explains little of that quarter.

  3. 3

    /commodities → ETF cross-sector

    GOLD vs PMGOLD vs MNRS β values. The two physical ETFs (GOLD, PMGOLD) are priced in AUD, so they should have β ≈ 1 to the AUD gold price; MNRS, an ETF of gold-mining shares, would be expected to show β > 1.

What the chart looks like

What this pattern means
Inference

When translated r is clearly higher than raw r, part of the apparent disconnect from gold is currency, not the equities. In a quarter where the AUD barely moved, as in Q1 2025, the wide spread between miners is more plausibly explained by company-specific factors such as costs, production and hedging.

Further context
Background

AUDUSD is one input to ASX gold-equity returns, because the USD gold price these companies receive is converted into AUD. RBA-vs-FOMC rate-path divergence is one widely watched driver of AUDUSD.

3

Is lithium recovering or just bouncing?

How do you tell a real recovery from a dead-cat bounce on a beaten-down basket?

▶
Catalyst

Lithium prices fell by more than 80% from their late-2022 peak. The question is how a real recovery would look different from a short bounce in the dashboard's data.

Walk-through — which panels to open

  1. 1

    /commodities/lithium → Today's setups + Full universe

    How many lithium names have MR ≥ 70 AND RSI ≥ 30? The 'AND ≥ 30' separates 'recovering' from 'still falling'. RSI < 25 with no tailwind = falling knife.

  2. 2

    /commodities → Cash flow (lithium row)

    14d/60d ratio. Lithium turning positive after a long negative stretch would be the data signature of a turn. Sparkline tells you whether it's gradual or stepped.

  3. 3

    /commodities/lithium → Strongest correlations vs ACDC ETF

    Rolling 90d r at lag 0. If correlation is breaking down (r dropping toward zero) the basket is decoupling from the ETF — evidence against a basket-wide recovery.

What the chart looks like

What this pattern means
Inference

Recovery vs bounce isn't binary. It's a triangulation across three independent confirmations: (a) MR + RSI rebound, (b) 14d cash flow turning sustained-positive, (c) basket correlation to ACDC ETF rising back toward normal. The more of the three that are present, the broader the evidence of a turn in the data; one alone is weak evidence.

Further context
Background

China EV sales and spodumene prices also matter, but the dashboard doesn't show them.

4

Which gold proxy is most predictive of ASX gold names?

Which single screen best leads ASX gold names — and does the answer change in different market conditions?

▶
Catalyst

Several candidates: GOLD ETF, PMGOLD ETF, MNRS ETF, gold spot, gold futures. Which one leads ASX gold equities?

Walk-through — which panels to open

  1. 1

    /commodities → ETF cross-sector

    Pairwise r for GOLD/PMGOLD/MNRS. GOLD and PMGOLD should be near-perfectly correlated; MNRS, an equity ETF of gold miners, less so.

  2. 2

    /commodities/gold → Strongest correlations

    Lead-lag bars for each gold proxy vs the gold-equity basket at ±5d. The proxy with highest r at lag +1 or +2 is the leader.

What the chart looks like

What this pattern means
Inference

GOLD and PMGOLD move almost together at lag 0; MNRS adds miner-specific moves on top of gold. Between proxies, the differentiator is LEAD-LAG. The lead-lag bars show which proxy, if any, has led over the window on screen.

Further context
Background

Which proxy leads, if any, can change over time; the dashboard shows only the current window.

5

Did sector flow flag the iron-ore drop?

When a macro shock hits a commodity, can the dashboard distinguish a signal that *led* the move from one that *reacted* to it?

▶
Catalyst

Weak Chinese steel demand weighed on iron ore in August–September 2024. An equal-weight basket of ASX iron-ore names (FMG, RIO, MIN, CIA, GRR, MGX) fell about 17% between 22 August and 10 September 2024 in the dashboard's price data. Did the flow data move first?

Walk-through — which panels to open

  1. 1

    /commodities → Cash flow (iron-ore row)

    Did 14d signed flow turn negative ahead of the move? If it did, iron ore would appear near the top of the outflows panel before the fall.

  2. 2

    /commodities/iron_ore → Vs commodity spot

    AUDUSD-translated β to iron-ore spot. Was the basket tracking spot tightly through the drop, or was AUD weakness cushioning the move?

What the chart looks like

What this pattern means
Inference

If iron-ore flow turned negative for several trading days BEFORE the fall, flow moved first. If it only turned ON or AFTER the fall, the flow was reacting to the price move rather than leading it.

Further context
Background

14-day flow is a slow-moving measure, so it reflects gradual drift (flows draining over weeks) better than sudden moves (geopolitical shocks, China policy announcements), which can outpace it.

6

China stimulus → copper rallies. Which ASX names lead?

When the macro print hits, which ASX copper names move first — and how do diversified miners compare with copper-focused names?

▶
Catalyst

Scenario: a China stimulus announcement (for example the PBoC package of 24 September 2024) lifts LME copper sharply over a few days. How does the ASX copper basket respond?

Walk-through — which panels to open

  1. 1

    /commodities/copper → Today's setups + Full universe

    Pre-catalyst: are any copper-focused names (e.g. 29M, AIS) already in the MR ≥ 70 zone? With less exposure to other commodities, they would be expected to respond more strongly to a copper move.

  2. 2

    /commodities/copper → Vs commodity spot

    β to the copper spot price. β measures how strongly a name responds to a copper move. Copper-focused names would be expected to show a higher β than diversified miners (BHP, S32), whose revenue also comes from other commodities.

  3. 3

    /commodities → Sector heat + Cash flow

    On D+1 after the print, was copper the brightest cell? A cash flow ratio above 2× suggests heavier-than-usual traded value alongside the move.

What the chart looks like

What this pattern means
Inference

A China stimulus announcement affects the whole basket, but names can respond very differently. Copper-focused names carry more copper exposure and more single-company risk; diversified miners' copper exposure is blended with their other commodities (iron ore, coal).

Further context
Background

β is estimated over a past window and changes over time; the dashboard shows the current window only.

7

Rare earths spike on China export controls — do the spikes persist?

What does the dashboard show about whether an announcement-driven spike was backed by prior flow?

▶
Catalyst

Scenario: Beijing announces gallium, germanium or rare-earth export licensing, and ASX rare-earth names (e.g. LYC, ILU, ARU, IXR) gap up at the open. What does the dashboard show about the spike?

Walk-through — which panels to open

  1. 1

    /commodities/rare_earths → Announcements panel

    Title-hit count for the day vs 60d baseline. A high title-hit count points to news about the commodity itself; a spike with few title hits is more likely attention that followed the price move.

  2. 2

    /commodities/rare_earths → Today's setups (after the gap)

    RSI of the basket the morning after. RSI > 75 with no fundamental delivery (production starts, contracts signed) over the next 5 days = a spike without fundamental follow-through.

  3. 3

    /commodities → Cash flow (rare-earths row, sparkline)

    14d signed flow direction. Positive flow in the days before the announcement means the news arrived on top of an existing trend; flat or negative flow means the gap came without prior accumulation.

  4. 4

    /commodities/rare_earths → Strongest correlations

    Correlation to MP Materials (US) and other non-ASX peers. If ASX names move and overseas peers don't, the move is more consistent with local positioning; a move shared with overseas peers reflects a global one.

What the chart looks like

What this pattern means
Inference

Announcement spikes can be split two ways: news that arrives on top of an existing positive flow trend, and news that triggers buying with no prior flow. The cash-flow sparkline separates the two; whether either kind persists is not something this page tests. Cross-listing symmetry (ASX vs US peer) is a second filter.

Further context
Background

Announcement ratio and 14d flow sign together give a 2×2 view of a spike.

8

Tin squeeze — only 3–4 ASX names. How do they compare?

With a basket of 3–4 names, basket-level statistics don't really apply. How do the individual tin names compare today?

▶
Catalyst

Scenario: the tin price spikes, for example on Indonesian export changes or the 2023 Wa State mining halt in Myanmar. The ASX tin universe is small: MLX, ELT, plus a couple of explorers.

Walk-through — which panels to open

  1. 1

    /commodities/tin → Full universe

    Sort by MR score. Cross-reference with RSI ≤ 30 AND drawdown column — the most stretched-down profile is RSI 20–30 + 30d drawdown < -15% + 52w drawdown < -25%.

  2. 2

    /commodities/tin → RSI vs drawdown scatter

    Bottom-left quadrant (low RSI + deep drawdown) = stretched-down names. Bottom-right (low RSI + shallow drawdown) = recently fast moves, more noise.

  3. 3

    /commodities/tin → Strongest correlations

    Correlation to LME tin (translated through AUDUSD). Names with β > 1 to tin spot get the leverage; β < 0.5 means the equity is decoupled from the commodity (probably for fundamental reasons — read announcements first).

  4. 4

    /commodities/tin → Recent announcements list

    Dollar volume on individual announcement days. A high-volume announcement day with the price not moving much is sometimes read as absorption (buyers taking up the supply around the news).

What the chart looks like

What this pattern means
Inference

Thin baskets lose the law-of-large-numbers benefit of a basket — every name is single-name idiosyncratic. The question shifts from 'is the commodity setup right' to 'is THIS company financially sound.' The MR + drawdown statistics still describe each name, but company announcements carry more of the information.

Further context
Background

Basket-level statistics become more meaningful as the number of liquid names grows.

9

Silver and gold — how does the lead-lag show up?

How do the gold–silver lead-lag and the gold-silver ratio show up on the dashboard?

▶
Catalyst

Scenario: gold rallies 10%+ in a quarter. Does silver follow, and with what delay? The gold-silver ratio (gold price ÷ silver price) shows whether silver is keeping up.

Walk-through — which panels to open

  1. 1

    /commodities/silver → Vs commodity spot

    Translated correlation of the ASX silver basket (e.g. SVL plus explorers) to silver spot. A rising r means the basket is tracking the silver price more closely.

  2. 2

    /commodities → Sector heat + Cash flow

    Silver basket return vs gold basket return today. A catch-up shows up as the silver basket outperforming the gold basket after a period when gold led.

What the chart looks like

What this pattern means
Inference

If the lead-lag peak sits at a positive lag, gold has led the silver basket over that window. Any such lag describes the window measured, not a fixed rule, and it does not appear in every gold rally.

Further context
Background

The gold-silver ratio tracks the relationship: a falling ratio means silver is outperforming gold; a rising ratio means silver is lagging.

Markets / instruments

AP
Authorised Participant — Big bank/market-maker permitted to create or redeem ETF units directly with the issuer in exchange for the underlying basket.
ASX
Australian Securities Exchange — The Sydney-based exchange where Australian-listed companies trade. The canetoad universe is ASX-only.
ASX 200
S&P/ASX 200 Index — Top 200 ASX-listed companies by market cap. Treated as 'the market' for benchmarking.→ details
CDI
CHESS Depositary Interest — ASX-listed instrument that represents legal title to shares of a foreign-domiciled company. AAI (Alcoa) trades on the ASX as CDIs.
CHESS
Clearing House Electronic Subregister System — ASX's electronic register that records share ownership. Replaced paper certificates.
ETF
Exchange-Traded Fund — Single tradeable ticker holding a basket of underlying assets — physical commodity, sector index, currency, etc.→ details
IPO
Initial Public Offering — First time a private company sells shares to the public market.
LME
London Metal Exchange — Global benchmark venue for industrial metal futures (copper, aluminium, zinc, nickel, lead, tin).
M&A
Mergers and Acquisitions — Corporate transactions where companies combine or one buys another.
NAV
Net Asset Value — Per-unit value of a fund's holdings minus its liabilities. ETF prices stay close to NAV via the AP creation/redemption mechanism.
NYSE
New York Stock Exchange — Major US exchange.

Trading / strategy

ATR
Average True Range — Average size of the daily price range. A gauge of typical daily movement.→ details
bps
Basis Points — 1 bp = 0.01%. 100 bps = 1%. Used for fees, interest-rate moves, and small percentage changes.
CGT
Capital Gains Tax — Australian tax on the profit when you sell a capital asset (shares, property). 50% discount applies if held over 12 months by an individual.
IS
In-Sample — Data used to design/calibrate a strategy. Compare with OOS.
L-S / L/S
Long-Short — Strategy that simultaneously goes long some assets and short others, often to be market-neutral.→ details
MR
Mean Reversion — The tendency for prices that drift far from a recent average to move back toward it. Canetoad's main analytical framework.→ details
MR score
Mean-reversion score (canetoad) — Composite 0–100 score combining RSI + drawdown + falling-knife filter. Higher = more stretched-down.→ details
MtM / MTM
Mark-to-Market — Re-pricing a position at the current market price to compute its current value or unrealised P&L.
OOS
Out-of-Sample — Data NOT used to design a strategy — used to test whether it actually generalises. The honest test of a backtest.→ details
P&L
Profit and Loss — Running tally of money made or lost. Realised = closed positions; unrealised = open positions marked to market.
PB / P/B
Price-to-Book ratio — Share price divided by book value per share. Below 1 = market values the company at less than its accounting net worth.
PE / P/E
Price-to-Earnings ratio — Share price divided by earnings per share. Quick valuation heuristic.
RSI
Relative Strength Index — 0–100 oversold/overbought score over 14 trading days. ≤30 = stretched-down; ≥70 = stretched-up.→ details
TWAP
Time-Weighted Average Price — Average of prices over a time window, equally weighted by time. Sister to VWAP (which weights by volume).
VWAP
Volume-Weighted Average Price — Average price weighted by trade size. A common execution-quality benchmark.
YTD
Year-to-Date — Performance/return measured from 1 January of the current year to today.

Statistics

R²
R-squared (coefficient of determination) — Fraction of variance explained by a regression. R² = 0 means no relationship; R² = 1 means perfect fit.
Sharpe
Sharpe Ratio — Annualised return ÷ annualised volatility. The standard risk-adjusted score for a strategy.→ details
z-score
Z-score — Number of standard deviations a value sits above (positive) or below (negative) its rolling baseline.→ details

FX / macro

AEDT
Australian Eastern Daylight Time — UTC+11. In effect Oct → Apr (Sydney/Melbourne). ASX market hours 10:00–16:00 AEDT.
AEST
Australian Eastern Standard Time — UTC+10. In effect Apr → Oct (Sydney/Melbourne). ASX market hours 10:00–16:00 AEST.
AUD
Australian Dollar — Reporting currency on canetoad.
AUDUSD
Australian Dollar / US Dollar exchange rate — How many US dollars one Australian dollar buys (e.g. 0.65 AUDUSD = 1 AUD costs 65 US cents).→ details
FOMC
Federal Open Market Committee — The committee inside the US Federal Reserve that sets US interest-rate policy. Meets ~8 times a year.
FX
Foreign Exchange — Currency markets / currency translation.
RBA
Reserve Bank of Australia — Australia's central bank. Sets the cash rate. Australian equivalent of the Fed / FOMC.
USD
US Dollar — Quote currency for most globally-traded commodities (gold, copper, oil).
UTC
Coordinated Universal Time — Reference time without DST. Sydney = UTC+10 (AEST) or UTC+11 (AEDT).
VIX
CBOE Volatility Index — Implied volatility of the S&P 500 from options pricing. Readings above 20 are commonly read as elevated stress, above 30 as high stress.

Regulatory & filings

ACN
Australian Company Number — 9-digit ID issued to every Australian company.
ARSN

Markets basics

terms you'll see on any trading platform
Share / stock / equity
A small ownership slice of a company that trades on a stock exchange.Buying a share makes you a part-owner. The price moves with what other people are willing to pay. The Australian Securities Exchange (ASX) is the venue where ASX-listed shares trade.Example: BHP Group is one of Australia's largest companies. One BHP share is one slice of BHP.
Ticker
A short letter code that uniquely identifies a listed company.Example: BHP, NST, PDN, FMG. ASX codes are usually three characters and can include digits (e.g. 29M). The same letters can refer to different companies on different exchanges, which is why the canetoad universe is ASX-only.
Close / open price
Open is the first traded price of the day; close is the last. The dashboard mostly uses close prices.Example: BHP opens at A$45.20 on Monday and closes at A$45.80. The 1-day return is +1.3%.
Volume
How many shares changed hands during the day. Higher volume = more interest, more liquidity.Example: If a stock usually trades about 1 million shares a day, a 5 million-share day is unusual activity.
Dollar volume
price × volume — the total dollars that traded that day.More useful than raw volume because it normalises across different price levels. A penny stock trading 10 million shares at 5 cents is A$500K of activity; a large-cap trading 1 million shares at A$50 is A$50M.
Return (1d / 5d / 20d)
Percentage change in price over N trading days. The dashboard uses 1, 5, 20, and 60 days.Example: Close of A$50 today, A$48 yesterday → 1d return = +4.2%. Close of A$50 today, A$45 a month ago (~20 trading days) → 20d return = +11.1%.
ASX 200
Index of the 200 largest listed Australian companies, weighted by market cap. Treated as 'the market' for benchmarking.Example: If a portfolio gained 8% while the ASX 200 gained 5%, its excess return vs the benchmark is +3%.
Sector / commodity family
Group of tickers exposed to the same underlying commodity or industry.On canetoad, each commodity (gold, copper, lithium, uranium, etc.) forms a family. A single ticker can belong to multiple families if its operations span them.Example: South32 (S32) is tagged with manganese, alumina, and aluminium because it produces all three.→ /commodities — every panel is grouped by family.
ETF
Exchange-Traded Fund. A single tradeable ticker that holds a basket of underlying assets — physical commodity, a sector of stocks, a currency, etc.On canetoad we use ETFs as 'cleaner' proxies for commodity exposure than equity baskets, because ETF prices are less polluted by single-stock noise.Example: GOLD (Global X Physical Gold, formerly ETFS Physical Gold) tracks the spot price of gold in AUD. PMGOLD (Perth Mint Gold) does the same with different mechanics.
Market cap
Share price × total shares on issue. The dollar value the market puts on the entire company.ASX-materials sector is roughly: large-cap (> A$5B), mid-cap (A$1–5B), small-cap (A$200M–1B), micro-cap (A$50–200M), nano-cap (< A$50M). Smaller caps move further on the same news but have higher volatility and lower liquidity.
Liquidity
How easily you can buy or sell a position without moving the price. Function of average daily dollar volume.The canetoad universe filter is avg daily $vol ≥ A$50K, which is light. On thinly traded names, slippage (the gap between quoted and filled prices) becomes large relative to typical moves.→ /ranker → average daily dollar volume column.

Technical indicators

quantitative descriptions of price behaviour
RSI(14)
Relative Strength Index over 14 trading days. A 0–100 score for whether a stock is overbought or oversold.Calculated from the ratio of average up-day moves to average down-day moves over 14 days. The standard interpretation: ≤30 oversold, ≥70 overbought, 40–60 neutral.Example: RSI of 22 = stretched-down. RSI of 78 = stretched-up.→ /commodities → MR leaderboard uses an RSI ≤ 25 threshold.
Drawdown
How far below a recent peak the price currently sits, as a percentage.The dashboard uses 30-day high (short window) and 52-week high (long window). 'How fast it fell' is RSI; 'how far it fell' is drawdown — the two together describe the stretched-down setup.Example: Stock made a 52-week high of A$2.00 six months ago, now trades at A$1.30. 52w drawdown = -35%.
Mean reversion
The idea that prices that drift far from a recent average tend to move back toward it.The hypothesis is that it shows up mainly over short horizons, when the move was caused by sentiment rather than by a change in fundamentals. The canetoad MR leaderboard ranks the most stretched-down tickers using RSI + drawdown + falling-knife filter.Example: A small-cap drops 25% over a week on no fundamental news. RSI hits 18. If it then partly recovers over the following days, that's mean reversion.→ /commodities → Mean reversion leaderboard, and the MR score column on every per-commodity table.
Falling-knife filter
A check that excludes names which are 'oversold' only because they're still trending down hard.Without this filter, the lowest-RSI list would include names still in steep decline, including companies in genuine difficulty. The filter looks at the 52-week trend and removes names whose long-term direction is still strongly negative.Example: A stock down 70% on the year with RSI 12 isn't oversold-and-bouncing — it's likely structurally broken. The filter excludes it.
Volatility
How much a price typically moves day-to-day. Higher volatility = bigger swings in both directions.Measured as the standard deviation of daily returns over 20 days. The 20-day volatility column on /ranker is this metric.
Correlation (r)
A score from -1 to +1 measuring how two price series move together.+1 = perfect lockstep, 0 = no relationship, -1 = perfectly opposite.Example: Two physical gold ETFs such as GOLD and PMGOLD have r close to +1, because they hold the same metal. A gold miner and a uranium miner would be expected to have a much lower r, because their businesses are driven by different prices.
Beta (β)
How much one series moves per 1% move in another. Slope of a regression of A onto B.If a stock has β=1.2 to gold, it moves 1.2% for every 1% move in gold (on average). β=0 means no sensitivity. Used both as a measure of exposure and for hedging.Example: Gold miners often have β > 1 to the gold price because much of their cost base is fixed (operational leverage).
Lead-lag correlation
Correlation measured at offset days — does A predict B's move tomorrow, or does B predict A's?If r between A_today and B_tomorrow is high, A leads B by 1 day. The /commodities/[name] correlations panel charts this from -30 to +30 days.Example: Gold keeps trading in London and New York after the ASX closes, so part of a gold move can reach ASX gold miners only on the next trading day. Against a USD gold price series, that would show up as a peak at lag +1.→ /commodities/[name] → Strongest correlations panel (±30d lag bars).
Z-score
How many standard deviations a value sits above (positive) or below (negative) its rolling baseline.Useful for normalising a metric so different commodities are comparable. The cash-flow z-score takes today's signed dollar flow, subtracts the trailing 60-day mean, divides by 60-day stddev. Result: a unit-free number that says 'today is +2σ above the recent typical day' or '−1σ below'.Example: If a commodity's typical 14-day flow is +A$100M with stddev A$50M, today's reading of +A$300M is z = (300−100)/50 = +4 — extremely elevated.
ATR (Average True Range)
Average size of the daily price range — typical daily move, in absolute dollars or as a percentage of price.Used to judge what counts as a routine daily move versus an unusual one.Example: Stock with 20-day ATR of 4c on a A$1 base = ~4% typical daily move; a 2c move is within routine noise.
Granger causality
A formal statistical test for whether one time series helps predict another beyond what its own past predicts.Doesn't prove causation in a philosophical sense, but answers the practical question 'does adding A's history to a model of B reduce B's forecast error?'

Sector heat

Translating returns through AUDUSD strips that noise out.Example: Illustrative: if raw r is ~0.05 and translated r is ~0.37, most of the gap is FX, not the equities.→ /commodities/[name] → Vs commodity spot (raw vs translated columns).
Today's setups
Per-commodity panel that surfaces tickers where MR ≥ 70 AND RSI ≤ 25 AND the falling-knife filter clears today.The shortlist of names that meet all three conditions simultaneously. An empty panel means no name meets all three today.→ /commodities/[name] → Today's setups.

Strategy

terms used in trading-decision and backtest contexts
Long / short
Long = you own the share and profit if it rises. Short = you've borrowed and sold the share, profit if it falls.Long is the default position. Shorting requires a margin account and pays interest on the borrowed shares. The dashboard's statistics focus on oversold (stretched-down) names.
Position sizing
How much capital you commit to a single trade.Practitioners often cap any single position at a fixed percentage of capital and scale size down for higher-volatility names, so the dollar risk is comparable across positions.
Stop-loss / take-profit
Pre-decided exit prices: a lower one (stop-loss) to cap losses, an upper one (take-profit) to lock in gains.Example: a position opened at A$1.00 with a stop at A$0.92 (-8%) and a take-profit at A$1.20 (+20%) closes at whichever level is reached first.
Holding period
How long you intend to hold a position before exit.Holding period matters because trading costs weigh more heavily on short holds. Day trade = same session. Swing = 1–5 days. Position = 1–4 weeks. Investment = months to years.
Sharpe ratio
Annualised return divided by annualised volatility — risk-adjusted return.A common rule of thumb: around 1.0 is good for an equity strategy, 2.0 is excellent, and 3.0 or more in a backtest is suspicious (often overfit or hiding tail risk).
Alpha
Excess return over a benchmark (usually a market index), unrelated to the benchmark's own moves.Calculated as the intercept of a regression of strategy returns on benchmark returns. Positive alpha = the strategy generated value beyond the market exposure. Annualised by ×252.Example: Strategy returned +18% with β=0.3 to ASX 200, ASX 200 returned +8%. Pure-market component is 0.3 × 8% = +2.4%. Alpha = 18% − 2.4% = +15.6%.
Decile sort
Rank-based portfolio test. Sort all candidates by signal strength, split into 10 equal buckets (deciles), then track the next-period return per bucket.If decile 10 (highest signal) outperforms decile 1 (lowest), the signal is informative. The D10 − D1 return gap is the long-short spread.
Slippage
The gap between the price you wanted and the price you actually got. Bigger on small-caps.On a thinly traded small-cap the bid-ask spread can be several percent, and a large order can move the price further (market impact).
Out-of-sample (OOS) test
Testing a strategy on data not used to design it — the only honest read on whether a backtested result is real or curve-fit.Standard split: first 70% of the dataset to design (in-sample), last 30% to test (out-of-sample). If OOS performance collapses vs in-sample, the strategy is overfit.

Mineral associations & deposit types

which commodities are commonly found together, and why · helps when reading announcements (a 'lithium' explorer reporting tantalum isn't surprising — it's just LCT pegmatite geology)
Why this is on the dashboard

Most ASX juniors get text-tagged into a single commodity bucket (canetoad does this). But the underlying geology means the same drillhole often produces multiple commodities. Knowing the deposit type tells you what by-products to expect — which helps when reading announcements (an epithermal Au system reporting antimony is normal, not a misclassification) and explains some cross-commodity links.

Major deposit types

12 types · the geological frameworks that explain why commodities co-occur

LCT pegmatite

Lithium-Cesium-Tantalum pegmatites — coarse-grained granitic intrusions enriched in incompatible elements.

▶

Late-stage residual melts from cooling granites concentrate elements that don't fit into common rock-forming minerals — lithium, cesium, tantalum, niobium, tin, beryllium. The lithium occurs as spodumene, petalite, or lepidolite; tantalum as tantalite/columbite; tin as cassiterite. A lithium pegmatite explorer often has by-product Ta + Sn exposure even if their announcements emphasise lithium.

Hostslithiumtantalumtinberylliumniobiumcesium
AU example: Greenbushes (WA), Wodgina (WA), Pilgangoora (WA)

IOCG (Iron Oxide Copper Gold)

Iron-rich hydrothermal systems hosting copper, gold, uranium, silver, and rare earths together.

▶

Olympic Dam-style deposits where iron-oxide-rich brines concentrate a polymetallic suite. The most multi-commodity deposit type in Australian geology: Olympic Dam produces Cu, U, Au and Ag from one orebody; REE are present but not recovered.

Hostscoppergolduraniumsilverrare earthsiron
AU example: Olympic Dam (SA), Carrapateena (SA), Prominent Hill (SA)

Porphyry (Cu-Au, Cu-Mo)

Large, low-grade copper systems associated with intrusive porphyry stocks; commonly with gold and/or molybdenum.

▶

Continental-arc settings (Andes, PNG, parts of NSW) host vast copper-gold or copper-molybdenum porphyries. Lower grade than IOCG (~0.5-1% Cu vs ~2%+) but enormous tonnage. Cu-Au porphyries are the gold byproduct dominant style; Cu-Mo are common in the western Americas. Australia has fewer than the Andes but Cadia (NSW) and several PNG systems are world-class.

Hostscoppergoldmolybdenumsilver
AU example: Cadia-Ridgeway (NSW), Northparkes (NSW), Frieda River (PNG)

Epithermal Au-Ag (and Au-Sb subtype)

Shallow hydrothermal vein systems forming high-grade gold and silver, sometimes with antimony or arsenic.

▶

Boiling fluids at shallow depths deposit gold + silver in narrow high-grade veins. The Au-Sb subtype (gold-antimony) is common where the system sits at the lower-temperature end — antimony precipitates at cooler temperatures, gold at the warmer parts. So an explorer reporting 'gold + antimony mineralisation' isn't surprising; gold-antimony is a common pairing in both epithermal and orogenic gold systems.

Hostsgoldsilverantimonyarsenicmercury
AU example: Cracow (Qld); Costerfield (Vic) and Hillgrove (NSW) are orogenic Au-Sb

Magmatic sulphide (Ni-Cu-PGE)

Sulphide-rich nickel deposits formed from segregation in mafic-ultramafic magmas; common with copper, cobalt, and platinum-group elements.

▶

When a sulphur-saturated mafic magma cools, sulphides settle out and concentrate nickel (the chalcophile element par excellence) along with copper, cobalt, and PGEs. The Kambalda nickel district (WA) and the Bushveld Complex (South Africa) are the type localities. A nickel sulphide explorer usually has some Cu + Co + PGE by-product exposure.

Hostsnickelcoppercobaltplatinum grouppalladium
AU example: Kambalda (WA), Mt Keith (WA), Nova-Bollinger (WA)

Carbonatite

Igneous rocks unusually rich in carbonate minerals; a major source of rare earths and niobium.

▶

Rare alkaline igneous rocks dominated by carbonate (calcite, dolomite) instead of silicate. Concentrate REE, Nb, Ta, P, and sometimes thorium and uranium. Mt Weld in WA is the type Australian example — a Lynas-mined REE deposit hosted in a weathered carbonatite. The Th/U content is a disposal challenge.

Hostsrare earthsniobiumtantalumphosphatethorium
AU example: Mt Weld (WA)

SEDEX / VHMS / MVT (Pb-Zn-Ag)

Three sediment- and volcanic-hosted styles that all produce lead, zinc, and silver together.

▶

SEDEX (sedimentary exhalative): brines on a seafloor (Mt Isa, McArthur River). VHMS (volcanic-hosted massive sulphide): seafloor venting near volcanic centres (Rosebery in Tasmania). MVT (Mississippi Valley-type): low-temperature carbonate-hosted (limited in Australia). All three produce Pb, Zn, Ag together with sometimes Cu and minor cadmium/indium. The Pb-Zn-Ag triplet is among the tightest commodity associations in geology.

Hostsleadzincsilvercoppercadmium
AU example: Mt Isa (Qld), McArthur River (NT), Rosebery (Tas), Cannington (Qld — Ag-Pb)

Banded iron formation (BIF)

Ancient sedimentary iron-silica layers; the source of essentially all bulk iron ore production.

▶

Mostly Archean-Proterozoic (~2-3.5 billion years old) — laid down when atmospheric oxygen first appeared. The Hamersley Province in WA is one of the world's great BIF belts. Iron ore is unusual in being a near-monoeconomic commodity: BIFs typically host only iron, with rare manganese co-occurrence and very occasional REE.

Hostsiron
AU example: Hamersley (Pilbara, WA) — Mount Whaleback, Tom Price

Laterite (Ni-Co, bauxite, Sc)

Tropical weathering profiles concentrating residual metals — nickel-cobalt-scandium, or aluminium (bauxite).

▶

When rocks weather in a humid tropical climate, soluble elements leach away and leave a residue. Over ultramafic rocks, Ni + Co + sometimes Sc concentrate in the saprolite zone; over aluminium-rich rocks, aluminium concentrates as bauxite. Lower grade than sulphide nickel but vast tonnage and lower mining cost. Many Indonesian/Philippine nickel mines are this style.

Hostsnickelcobaltscandiumbauxitealuminium
AU example: Murrin Murrin (WA), Ravensthorpe (WA), Weipa bauxite (Qld)

Sandstone-hosted uranium (and vanadium)

Roll-front uranium deposits in sandstones — uranium often paired with vanadium.

▶

Oxidising groundwater dissolves uranium, then re-precipitates it where it hits a reducing zone (often where organic matter or sulphides exist). Vanadium follows the same reduction-precipitation chemistry, so V is a common by-product. Honeymoon (SA) and Beverley (SA) are Australian examples of in-situ recovery (ISR) operations.

Hostsuraniumvanadium
AU example: Honeymoon (SA), Beverley (SA)

Granite-related Sn-W

Tin and tungsten deposits formed at the contact of tin-bearing granites with surrounding rocks.

▶

Sn and W tend to occur together because both partition similarly into late-stage granitic melts. Cassiterite (tin) and wolframite/scheelite (tungsten) form in skarns or greisens at the granite contact. Sometimes molybdenum joins the suite. Tasmania has the classic Australian Sn-W belt; Renison Bell is a major tin mine.

Hoststintungstenmolybdenum
AU example: Renison Bell (Tas), King Island scheelite (Tas)

Heavy mineral sands

Beach- and dune-derived placer deposits of titanium minerals plus zircon and monazite.

▶

Wave action on coastlines concentrates dense, weathering-resistant minerals — ilmenite, rutile, leucoxene (the titanium minerals), plus zircon and monazite (a REE mineral). Australia is one of the world's largest producers of mineral sands; Iluka Resources is the best-known ASX-listed producer.

Hoststitaniumzirconrare earthsthorium
AU example: Eneabba (WA), Murray Basin (Vic/NSW), Stradbroke Island (Qld — historical)

Helium accumulates in some natural-gas reservoirs as a decay product of uranium and thorium in basement rocks. Very localised — only a handful of fields globally produce commercial helium. Australia has minor production.
